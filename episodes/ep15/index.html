The "Lonely" Sleep Waves of the Aging Brain

Watch on YouTube

During deep sleep, large groups of neurons go quiet and switch back on together, and these slow waves travel across the brain from front to back. A UC Berkeley team compared overnight EEG in 61 young and 75 older adults. In the older group, slow waves traveled about 15% less far and reached fewer recording sites; the authors call them "lonely" waves. Lonelier waves went with more tau protein in the frontal lobe (PET) and with weaker overnight memory, and in a separate Washington University cohort they went with a higher tau-to-amyloid ratio in spinal fluid.

Caveats
These are correlations, not proof of cause. Participants were older adults without dementia, the samples were small (roughly 35–80 per analysis), and effects were modest. Whether tau disrupts the waves, the waves affect tau, or something else drives both is unknown. The 15% figure is our calculation from the paper's published source data (group means, 145 vs 123 mm).
